## Overview

### Component Selection Process

This page documents the component selection process for the Gripper & Object Handling subsystem of Team 107's Simple Educational Robotic Arm.

The subsystem uses a PIC18F57Q43 Curiosity Nano microcontroller to control a servo-operated gripper and monitor gripping pressure through a Force Sensitive Resistor (FSR).

The selected gripper is the Kitronik Klaw Mk2 Robotic Gripper Kit (manufacturer part number 25104), which includes a servo motor for opening and closing the gripper.

The Interlink Electronics FSR 402 measures relative gripping pressure. Its signal is conditioned using an MCP6004 operational amplifier before being read by the microcontroller's analog-to-digital converter (ADC).

The subsystem uses an external 9 V DC input with an L7805 voltage regulator to provide a nominal 5 V supply for external electronics. The PIC18F57Q43 Curiosity Nano is powered separately through USB.

The component selection process prioritizes affordability, compatibility, simplicity, and the ability to handle lightweight objects.

The allocated budget for this subsystem is **$60**. The PIC18F57Q43 Curiosity Nano is already owned and is excluded from the purchasing budget.

The following sections compare component alternatives and explain how the selected hardware supports the robotic arm's product requirements.

### Final Design Decision: Object Detection Sensor Removed

**Original Candidate:** Vishay TCRT5000

**Sensor Type:** Reflective infrared proximity sensor

**Final Selection:** Not included in the revised design

**Design Decision Explanation:**

The original Gripper & Object Handling subsystem
included an infrared object detection sensor to
identify objects positioned near the gripper.

Three sensor alternatives were evaluated:
the Vishay TCRT5000, Vishay CNY70, and
ST VL53L0X.

After revising the subsystem design, the
dedicated infrared object detection sensor
was removed to reduce circuit complexity,
component count, and cost.

The final design uses the Interlink FSR 402
force sensor to monitor gripping pressure
when the gripper contacts an object.

The PIC18F57Q43 receives the conditioned
FSR signal through its ADC input and can
use this information to monitor gripping
pressure.

The FSR does not provide non-contact
object detection. Instead, it provides
feedback when force is applied.

Removing the infrared sensor simplifies
the hardware while maintaining the
subsystem's primary gripping and
force-monitoring functions.

**Final Status:** Infrared object detection
sensor removed from the final schematic.

### Selected Component: Interlink FSR 402

**Manufacturer:** Interlink Electronics

**Part Number:** FSR 402 (30-81794)

**Sensor Type:** Force-sensitive resistor

**Microcontroller Connection:** RA0/ANA0 analog input through a voltage divider


**Selection Explanation:**

The Interlink Electronics FSR 402 was selected
because it provides a lightweight and affordable
method of monitoring gripping pressure.

The FSR 402 changes resistance when force is
applied to its sensing surface. This resistance
change is converted into a voltage using a
voltage-divider circuit with a 10 kΩ resistor.

The voltage signal is conditioned using an
MCP6004 operational amplifier before being
sent to the PIC18F57Q43 microcontroller.

The conditioned analog signal is connected to
the PIC's RA0/ANA0 analog input, allowing the
ADC to monitor relative gripping pressure.

The MCP6004 was selected because it operates
from a single supply and provides a suitable
interface for buffering the force sensor signal.

The FSR 402 is intended for relative pressure
monitoring rather than precise force measurement.

Compared with the FSR 400 and TAL220 load cell,
the FSR 402 offers a practical balance of
cost, size, and circuit simplicity for an
educational robotic gripper.

The final sensor mounting arrangement and
gripping thresholds will be determined
through testing.

**Final Selected Components:**

- Force Sensor: Interlink Electronics FSR 402
- Part Number: 30-81794
- Signal Conditioning: MCP6004 operational amplifier
- Voltage Divider Resistor: 10 kΩ
- Microcontroller Input: RA0/ANA0 (ADC)

## Component Selection 5: Power Supply and Voltage Regulation

The Gripper & Object Handling subsystem requires regulated power for the PIC18F57Q43 microcontroller, object detection sensor, force sensor, and servo motor. The design uses a USB power source and must provide a stable supply voltage while maintaining a common ground between components.

Three voltage regulator options were compared based on cost, output current, efficiency, and ease of integration.

### Table 5: Voltage Regulator Comparison

| Solution | Advantages | Disadvantages | Reference Price |
|---|---|---|---|
| **ST L7805CV Linear Regulator** | Simple circuit; regulated 5 V output; widely available; low electrical noise | Requires sufficient input voltage above 5 V; dissipates heat; limited output current | Approximately $1–$2 |
| **Texas Instruments LM2940CT-5.0 Linear Regulator** | Regulated 5 V output; lower dropout than a traditional 7805; relatively simple circuit | Still requires input voltage above 5 V; dissipates heat; capacitor requirements must be followed | Approximately $2–$4 |
| **Pololu D24V22F5 Switching Regulator** | Regulated 5 V output; high efficiency; suitable for higher current loads | More expensive; switching noise; not a linear regulator | Approximately $10–$15 |

### Selected Regulation Approach

**External Power Input:** 9 V DC

**Input Protection:** 1 A fuse

**Selected Voltage Regulator:** ST L7805CV

**Regulated Output:** Nominal 5 V DC

**Microcontroller Power:** USB connection

**Servo Power:** External regulated 5 V rail

### Selection Explanation

The Gripper & Object Handling subsystem uses an
external 9 V DC power supply to provide power
for the gripper and associated electronics.

The 9 V input passes through a 1 A fuse before
reaching the L7805 linear voltage regulator.

The L7805 converts the external 9 V input into
a nominal 5 V output for the servo and external
sensor circuitry.

The PIC18F57Q43 Curiosity Nano is powered
separately through its USB connection.

A common electrical ground is required between
the PIC and the externally powered circuitry
to provide a reference for control and
sensor signals.

The L7805 was selected because it provides
a straightforward method of obtaining a
regulated 5 V output from a higher DC input.

The LM2940 and Pololu switching regulator
were also considered as alternatives.

The L7805 has the disadvantage of dissipating
the voltage difference between its input and
output as heat.

### Voltage Regulator Power Calculation

Input Voltage = 9 V
Output Voltage = 5 V

Voltage Drop = 9 V - 5 V = 4 V

Approximate Regulator Power Dissipation:

P_regulator = (V_in - V_out) × I_load

P_regulator = 4 V × I_load

For example, at a 500 mA output current:

P_regulator = 4 V × 0.5 A = 2 W

This is an illustrative calculation, not a
measured servo operating current.

### Design Verification Requirements

Before finalizing the hardware, the following
items must be verified:

- Servo operating and stall current
- External 9 V supply current rating
- L7805 maximum output current
- Regulator temperature and heatsinking
- Compatibility of the 5 V servo control signal
  with the PIC's actual GPIO voltage
- Correct common-ground connections

The 1 A fuse provides input overcurrent
protection but does not replace thermal
protection or regulator current verification.

**Final Selection Status:** L7805 selected
for the revised schematic. Current capacity
and thermal suitability remain pending
verification.
